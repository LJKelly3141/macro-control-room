October 28, 2026

Step 4 of specs/POLICY_UNIVERSES_OPTIMAL_POLICY_PLAN.md. Generated by node scripts/policy_universes_compare.js (12 s on this machine).

Approaches (all played in the true nonlinear engine, on the players’ own buttons):

  • No policy: rate held at 3%, no fiscal change.
  • Taylor rule: Mankiw’s rule (0.5, 0.5), the game’s autopilot.
  • LQ rule: the infinite-horizon linear-quadratic optimal feedback rule (discount 0.95, instrument costs 0.05 per spending step squared and 0.8 per rate point squared), rounded to the buttons.
  • Search: finite-horizon perfect-foresight coordinate search over the buttons, started from no policy, the Taylor path and the LQ path.

Scoring: total welfare loss \(\sum [w_u(u-5)^2 + w_\pi(\pi-2)^2]\) over the game plus 4 tail rounds with no new policy. Share of the achievable gain = \((L_\text{none} - L)/(L_\text{none} - L_\text{search})\): 100% matches the search, 0% is no better than doing nothing. Shares are reported only where the achievable gain is above 0.5 loss points (180 of 180 cases).

Cases: 5 presets x 4 events x 3 game lengths (8, 12, 20) x 3 welfare weightings = 180.

Findings

  1. The LQ rule is close to the best achievable. Median 92% of the achievable gain over 180 cases, against 54% for the Taylor rule. It did worse than doing nothing in 1 case of 180; the Taylor rule did in 42, mostly in the New Keynesian and neoclassical economies, where its fixed 0.5 weights over-react.

  2. The cost of linearization is concentrated where the plan expected.

    • The only failure is a long spell at the 0% rate floor (neoclassical, confidence collapses, 20 rounds, inflation weighted double). The linear rule cannot see the floor; applying it with the rate held at the floor and fiscal policy re-optimized given that (from the same solved quadratic) cut this case from -488% to -43% and removed two other failures, but a long spell at the floor still needs foresight that only the search has.
    • The other weak cases (59% to 70%) are the new-technology event, where doing nothing loses very little to begin with (achievable gains of 1 to 2 loss points), so small differences show up as large percentages.

  3. The search is the upper bound, and it is cheap. It beat the LQ rule in 167 of 180 cases, usually by a few percentage points of the achievable gain.

  4. Both run comfortably in the browser (Chrome, timed 2026-10-28, worst case over Keynesian, Monetarist and New Keynesian games after a demand drop or an oil spike, one universe):

    CPU Search, 12 rounds Search, 20 rounds LQ rule, any length
    This laptop 40 ms 95 ms under 5 ms
    4 times slower (a mid-range phone) 166 ms 410 ms under 20 ms
    6 times slower (an older phone) 246 ms 621 ms under 30 ms

    Two universes double the search time. Nothing needs to be precomputed.

Recommendation

Ship both, each in the role it is suited for:

  • The search as the “optimal” benchmark path and score (“share of the achievable gain”). It uses the true model, the 0% floor and the players’ own buttons, so 100% is genuinely the best the class could have done with perfect foresight. Computed at game start, behind the event card; in “I choose each round” mode, at the end of the game.
  • The LQ rule as “Autopilot: optimal rule”, beside the Taylor rule. It is a rule a central bank could actually follow (it reacts to the current state, with no foresight), it is derived from the loss function rather than assumed, and it beats the Taylor rule in most cases. Its weights can be shown beside Taylor’s 0.5 and 0.5.
  • Say plainly in the game that the optimal path assumes perfect foresight, and that the optimal rule is optimal for a simplified (linear) version of the model and can stumble at the 0% rate floor.

Open choices left to Logan are listed in the plan (discount 0.95, instrument costs, spending-only fiscal plan, 4 tail rounds); the comparison used the recommended values.
